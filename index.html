Advice for Patients During a Medicines Shortage

Information and guidance for patients and carers in England

We know how difficult and stressful medicines supply issues can be for patients and carers. This page provides practical advice about what to do if your medicine is unavailable and explains how pharmacies, GPs and the NHS work together to minimise the impact of shortages.

What You Should Do

  • Order your repeat prescription in good time while you still have medication available.
  • Speak to your pharmacist as early as possible if you are having difficulty obtaining your medicine.
  • Avoid stockpiling medicines or ordering more than you need.
  • Follow the advice given by your pharmacist, GP or specialist healthcare professional.

Why Do Medicines Shortages Happen?

Most prescription medicines are supplied without any problems. However, temporary shortages can occur. These may be caused by:

  • Shortages of ingredients needed to manufacture medicines.
  • Unexpected increases in demand.
  • Manufacturing or quality assurance issues.
  • Global events affecting supply chains.

The government, medicine suppliers and NHS organisations work closely together to manage shortages and reduce disruption to patient care.

Frequently Asked Questions

What will my pharmacy or GP do if my medication is unavailable because of a national shortage?

You may:

  • Be advised to collect your medicine from another pharmacy.
  • Receive a different formulation, such as tablets instead of capsules.
  • Receive a smaller quantity of medicine and need more frequent collections.
  • Be asked to obtain a new prescription for an available alternative medicine.
My pharmacy cannot get my medicine, but another pharmacy might have it. Why?

Different pharmacies use different suppliers and wholesalers. In addition, some medicines are subject to ordering limits (quotas), meaning one pharmacy may still have stock while another has exhausted its allowance.

Can my pharmacy see stock levels at other pharmacies?

Pharmacies that belong to different companies generally do not share computer systems and cannot directly see each other's stock levels. However, pharmacy teams may be able to contact nearby pharmacies to help locate supplies.

What if an alternative medicine causes side effects or does not work as well?

You should discuss this with your pharmacist or doctor. They can review your treatment and determine whether a different approach is needed.

What if there is no alternative medicine available?

In rare circumstances there may be no suitable alternative treatment. Your doctor or pharmacist will explain the options available and advise you on the next steps.

Will my pharmacist know when my medicine is back in stock?

NHS healthcare professionals have access to regularly updated medicines supply information. Your pharmacist may be able to advise when supplies are expected to return.

What is the government doing to manage medicine shortages?

Medicine suppliers are legally required to report shortages to the government. National processes are in place involving medicine suppliers, NHS England, regulators and the wider supply chain to minimise the impact on patients.

I Still Have Questions

If you are concerned about your medicine, speak to your pharmacist or doctor. If you need urgent advice when your GP practice is closed, contact NHS 111.

This information is intended for patients and carers in England and provides general guidance regarding medicines shortages. Individual advice from your healthcare professional should always take precedence.
