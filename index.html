Advice for Patients During a Medicines Shortage

Information and guidance for patients and carers in England

We understand that it can be worrying when a medicine you rely on is temporarily unavailable. Medicines shortages can happen for a variety of reasons and may affect pharmacies across the UK.

The NHS, manufacturers, wholesalers and pharmacies work together to minimise disruption to patient care. This page explains what to do if your medicine is affected by a shortage and answers some common questions.

What should I do?

  • Order repeat prescriptions in good time before you run out of medication.
  • Speak to your pharmacist as early as possible if you are having difficulty obtaining a medicine.
  • Do not stockpile medicines or request extra supplies unnecessarily.
  • Follow advice provided by your pharmacist, GP or specialist team.
  • Do not stop prescribed medication unless advised by a healthcare professional.

Why do medicines shortages occur?

Medicines shortages can occur for several reasons, including:

  • Manufacturing difficulties.
  • Quality assurance issues.
  • Shortages of raw ingredients.
  • Unexpected increases in demand.
  • Global supply chain disruptions.
  • Transport and distribution challenges.

Most shortages are temporary and healthcare professionals receive regular updates regarding national supply issues and recommended alternatives.

Frequently Asked Questions

What happens if my medicine is unavailable?

Your pharmacist or prescriber may be able to:

  • Source stock from an alternative supplier.
  • Direct you to another pharmacy that has stock available.
  • Supply a different strength or formulation.
  • Arrange an alternative medicine where clinically appropriate.
Why can one pharmacy obtain my medicine when another cannot?

Pharmacies often use different suppliers and wholesalers. Some medicines are also subject to manufacturer quotas which can affect local availability.

Can pharmacies see stock held by other pharmacies?

Usually not. Different pharmacy organisations use different computer systems and cannot routinely view each other's stock levels.

Will I need a new prescription for an alternative medicine?

Sometimes. If a clinically suitable alternative is required, a GP or other authorised prescriber may need to issue a new prescription.

What if the alternative medicine causes side effects?

If you experience side effects or have concerns about an alternative medicine, please contact your pharmacist, GP practice or specialist team.

What if there is no suitable alternative available?

In rare circumstances there may not be a suitable alternative. Your healthcare team will discuss the available options and develop an individual treatment plan with you.

Will my pharmacist know when supplies will return?

Pharmacists receive regular supply updates and may be able to advise when stocks are expected to be available again.

What is being done nationally to manage medicines shortages?

Manufacturers, wholesalers, regulators and NHS organisations work together to identify and manage shortages in order to minimise disruption to patients.

Need further advice?

If you have concerns about your medicines, please contact:

  • Your community pharmacy
  • Your GP practice
  • Your hospital specialist team (if applicable)
  • NHS 111 for urgent advice when your usual healthcare services are unavailable
